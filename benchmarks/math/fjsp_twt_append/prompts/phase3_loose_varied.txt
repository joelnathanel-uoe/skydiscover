You are an expert in combinatorial optimization and scheduling. Your task
is to design a novel APPENDING CONSTRUCTION HEURISTIC for the Flexible Job
Shop Scheduling Problem with Total Weighted Tardiness (FJSP-TWT).

--- The problem ---

A set of jobs must be processed on a set of machines. Each job is an
ordered sequence of operations; each operation can be processed by one or
more eligible machines, with a processing time that may depend on the
machine. Each job has a release date, a due date, and a weight. Machines
process one operation at a time and operations are not interruptible.
Minimise total weighted tardiness:
  TWT = sum_j  w_j * max(0, C_j - d_j)
where w_j is the job weight, d_j its due date, C_j its completion time.

--- The appending scheme ---

A construction heuristic builds a complete schedule step by step,
scheduling one operation per step. The chosen operation is placed at the
END of the chosen machine's current sequence and started as early as
possible. You never choose a position: the harness — a fixed scheduling
loop that calls your function — always appends the operation you pick and
starts it as early as possible.

--- Anatomy of a construction step ---

Every step has four stages. THE HARNESS PERFORMS THE FIRST AND THE LAST.
YOU DESIGN THE TWO IN BETWEEN, AND NOTHING ELSE.

  1. CANDIDATE GENERATION — performed by the harness. The available
     operations are the next unscheduled operations of jobs whose preceding
     operations have all been scheduled. Each available operation o, paired
     with an eligible machine m, forms a candidate with earliest start
     s(o,m) = max(job ready time, machine free time) and earliest
     completion e(o,m) = s(o,m) + p(o,m), p(o,m) being the processing time
     of operation o using machine m. The finished set is handed to you.
  2. RESTRICTION — WHO COMPETES — yours (design choice 1). A rule that
     reduces the candidate set to a subset before any preference is
     expressed.
  3. SELECTION — WHO WINS — yours (design choice 2). A decision function
     that picks exactly one candidate from that subset.
  4. UPDATE — performed by the harness. The candidate you returned is
     appended to its machine at its earliest start, and ready times are
     updated for the next step.

So the whole of your contribution is: you receive a set of candidates and
you return one of them. Constructing the candidates, appending the winner,
computing start times and maintaining the schedule all happen outside your
function, and you cannot influence them.

Your appending heuristic is therefore fully described by its RESTRICTION RULE
and its SELECTION FUNCTION. Your heuristic must be expressible in these
terms, and you must report it in these terms — see "What to write" below.

The two sections that follow set out mechanisms available at the
restriction and selection stages. Your heuristic may incorporate any of
them, in any combination, and may use a mechanism neither section names.

--- Design choice 1: Restriction, who competes ---

The simplest choice is no restriction: the selection function sees all
candidates. Restriction matters for two reasons. First, it bounds the class
of schedules the heuristic can reach. Second, it caps the damage a weak
selection function can do: with a tight restriction even a poor score
chooses among few, structurally reasonable options, while with the full set
the score bears the entire burden. Restriction strength and selection
intelligence trade off against each other.

Two classic restrictions are worth knowing as reference points:

  * Targeting ACTIVE schedules. Find the candidate with the minimum
    earliest completion c*, attained on machine m*. Keep only candidates on
    m* that can start strictly before c*. Every schedule built this way is
    active — no operation can be completed earlier without delaying another
    — and the class of active schedules is guaranteed to contain an optimal
    schedule, for any objective non-decreasing in completion times, as this
    one is.
  * Targeting NON-DELAY schedules. Keep only candidates that can start at
    the overall minimum earliest start s*. No machine is ever left idle
    when it could work. Non-delay schedules are a strict subset of active
    schedules; the subset is not guaranteed to contain an optimum, and
    whether it is rich in good solutions depends on how valuable deliberate
    idleness is on the instance at hand.

These are instances of a general concept, not the whole menu. Other
mechanisms worth exploring, and you may invent your own:

  * Critical-machine focus: restrict to candidates on a focal machine
    chosen by a criterion other than earliest completion — e.g. largest
    remaining workload, or largest aggregate urgency of the jobs headed
    to it.
  * Score-band filtering: rank candidates by a cheap criterion and keep
    those within a tolerance of the best; decide among survivors with a
    richer criterion.
  * Urgency filtering with fallback: keep only candidates of urgent jobs
    (e.g. small slack — due date minus a reference time minus remaining
    work), falling back to the full set when none qualifies.

--- Design choice 2: Selection, who wins ---

Selection is a decision function that scores every candidate in the
restricted set and dispatches the best. What the literature calls a
"priority rule" and what one might call an "evaluation criterion" are the
same object. Rules can be characterised as static vs dynamic (does the
score change as the schedule grows?), myopic vs look-ahead (does it
anticipate consequences?), and local vs global (does it consider only the
candidate or all jobs?). The function may score (operation, machine) pairs
jointly or decompose hierarchically — operation first then machine, or the
reverse. The main options:

  * Myopic indices, the classical static dispatching rules: score by the
    candidate's own attributes — processing time, weight-to-processing-time
    ratio, earliest start or completion.
  * Due-date-aware dispatching rules: bring due-date information into the
    score, e.g. through slack or an urgency measure that grows as the due
    date approaches.
  * Estimates of the objective function, or of a surrogate of it:
    hypothetically dispatch the candidate, estimate the consequences for
    job completions, and score by the resulting objective value. How the
    consequences are estimated is itself a design choice. Note that the
    true objective is weakly informative during most of the construction —
    tardiness is flat at zero for every job still projected on time — so
    estimating an INFORMATIVE SURROGATE instead, a quantity that
    discriminates before jobs become tardy yet agrees with the objective
    where it matters, is a central creative opportunity.

Ties are frequent. The tie-breaking hierarchy is part of the design and
must be specified explicitly.

--- What to write ---

Your task is to implement choose_next. Each call it receives that set of
candidates and returns one of them:

    def choose_next(candidates, machine_sequences, machine_free, job_ready,
                    job_next_op, instance):
        ...
        return one_candidate

Structure its body as the two design choices, in this order, with the two
stages clearly separated and commented:

    # --- Restriction: who competes ---
    competing = ...        # a non-empty subset of candidates
    # --- Selection: who wins ---
    return ...             # exactly one element of competing

If your design applies no restriction, say so explicitly and pass the full
candidate set through. Do not fuse the two stages into one expression.

Begin the function with a docstring reporting the design in these terms:

    RESTRICTION: one sentence naming the rule.
    SELECTION:   one sentence naming the scoring function.
    PARAMETERS:  every constant your design uses and what it means,
                 or "none".

--- Arguments ---

  candidates        list of Candidate namedtuples, one per (available
                      operation, eligible machine) pair, in deterministic
                      order. Never empty. Fields:
                        c.op       (j, o) — job index, operation index
                        c.machine  machine id
                        c.start    earliest start s(o,m)
                        c.end      earliest completion e(o,m)
                        c.proc     processing time p(o,m)
  machine_sequences dict m -> [(op, start, end), ...] already committed on
                      machine m, in processing order
  machine_free      dict m -> time machine m becomes free (0 if unused)
  job_ready         dict j -> time job j becomes ready: the end of its last
                      committed operation, or its release time if none
  job_next_op       dict j -> index of j's next unscheduled operation, or
                      None if j is fully scheduled
  instance          n_jobs, n_machines, jobs[{release_time, due_date,
                      weight, operations: [[[machine_id, proc_time], ...],
                      ...]}]

The only decision you make is WHICH CANDIDATE IS SCHEDULED NEXT. Return
one of the candidates you were given, unmodified. Anything that does not
match one of the offered candidates is rejected and the program fails.
Concretely, this means you cannot invent a start time, pick a machine that
was not offered for that operation, or schedule an operation whose
predecessor is still unscheduled — none of those appear among your
candidates.

--- The instances ---

Every instance you are scored on has loose due dates, and the processing
time of an operation varies widely across the machines eligible for it.

--- Scoring ---

On each instance we compute

  score = (ref_twt - your_twt) / ref_twt

where ref_twt is the total weighted tardiness achieved on that instance by a
strong appending construction heuristic used as a fixed reference. A score of
0 means you matched it, a positive score means lower tardiness than it, and a
negative score means worse. If your function errors or exceeds its time
budget on any instance, the program scores -100.

Your score is the median over the instances. Your objective is to make this
score as high as you can. Subject to that, keep the design simple: use as few
parameters as possible, and make every parameter you do use interpretable —
a quantity with a clear scheduling meaning rather than an opaque tuning
constant. Between two designs that schedule comparably well, the one whose
behaviour can be explained is the better design.

--- Constraints ---

choose_next is called once per operation.

No state between calls: each call receives a fresh copy of instance —
anything written to it is lost. Setting attributes on the function itself
(e.g. choose_next._cache = ...) is forbidden and invalidates the program.
Make each decision using only the arguments passed in that call.

Each call has a time budget proportional to the number of candidates at
that step.

Restrictions: Python standard library + numpy only.
